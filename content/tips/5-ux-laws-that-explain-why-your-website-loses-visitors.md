<p>UX laws sound like something you'd only care about if you had a computer science degree. You don't need one. These five principles explain almost every bad website decision ever made — and once you know them, you can't unsee them.</p><h2>1. Hick's Law — too many choices = no choice</h2><p>The more options you give someone, the longer they take to decide. Often they just leave. This is why your navigation should have five or six items max, and why "just add more services to the homepage" is almost always wrong.</p><h2>2. Miller's Law — the magic number is 7 (ish)</h2><p>People can hold about 7 things in working memory at once, give or take 2. If your pricing page has 12 features listed per plan, nobody's reading them. Chunk your content. Group things. Use headers.</p><h2>3. The Fold — people do scroll, but not always</h2><p>"Above the fold" means what people see before they scroll. Your most important message — who you are, what you do, why someone should care — needs to be there. Not buried three sections down.</p><h2>4. Fitts's Law — make your buttons bigger</h2><p>The time it takes to click something depends on how far away it is and how big it is. Your call-to-action button should not be tiny, grey, and in the corner.</p><h2>5. Jakob's Law — people expect your site to work like every other site</h2><p>Users spend most of their time on other websites. They've learned patterns — logo top left, nav across the top, footer at the bottom. Fighting those patterns is creative. It's also confusing. Save the creativity for your visuals.</p>
